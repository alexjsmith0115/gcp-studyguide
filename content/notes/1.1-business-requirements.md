## Why it matters

Many exam scenarios start with business facts, not technical specifications. You turn those facts into requirements, rank them, and choose the design that meets the hard limits at the lowest cost and risk. The exam tests three skills: tell a real requirement from a preference, choose a disposition for each workload, and define measures of success.

## Key points

- A Well-Architected Framework pillar covers one non-functional focus area: security, reliability, performance, cost, operations, or sustainability ([Well-Architected Framework](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework)).
- Start from the targeted business use case and its objectives. Agree on a vision statement and get stakeholder sign-off before you plan the architecture ([Plan a hybrid and multicloud strategy](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/hybrid-multicloud-patterns/strategy)).
- Requirements can differ between components, operations, and time periods. Assess them granularly, so that you spend money on the parts that are critical for the business ([Assess the reliability requirements](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/infra-reliability-guide/requirements)).
- Non-functional requirements interact. For example, cost quotas and data residency rules limit the reliability designs that you can use ([Assess the reliability requirements](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/infra-reliability-guide/requirements)).
- Google defines six migration types: rehost, replatform, refactor, re-architect, rebuild, and repurchase ([Migrate to Google Cloud: Get started](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-getting-started)). Migration Center planning also lists retire as a path ([About migration planning](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/migration-planning-overview)).
- Design a system to be just reliable enough for user happiness. A goal of 100% reliability is often not the most effective strategy ([Set realistic targets for reliability](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/reliability/set-targets)).
- Measure reliability from the user's view, for example the success ratio of user requests. Do not rely only on server metrics such as CPU usage ([Define reliability based on user-experience goals](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/reliability/define-reliability-based-on-user-experience-goals)).
- Decide on measurable success criteria for a migration, with clear milestones for each phase or wave ([Plan a hybrid and multicloud strategy](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/hybrid-multicloud-patterns/strategy)).
- Disaster recovery (DR) is a subset of business continuity planning. A business impact analysis defines the recovery time objective (RTO) and the recovery point objective (RPO) ([Disaster recovery planning guide](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/dr-scenarios-planning-guide)).
- A total cost of ownership (TCO) model includes the operational costs of your data center, such as power, cooling, and maintenance. Network use is a commonly overlooked cloud cost ([Assess and discover your workloads](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)).

## From a scenario to requirements

Use this method on every scenario and case study.

1. Find the business use case and the objective. Ask why the current environment does not meet the objective, and what the cloud must improve.
2. List what the system must do. These are the functional requirements.
3. List how well the system must do it: availability, latency, scale, recovery, and security. These are the non-functional requirements. Change vague words ("always available", "fast") into numbers with a measurement window.
4. List the constraints: regulations, data location, licenses, existing systems, skills, time, and budget. A constraint comes from outside the design, so you cannot trade it away.
5. List the success measures: KPIs, ROI targets, and SLOs.
6. Remove each option that breaks a constraint. Then use the qualifier in the question ("most cost-effective", "least operational overhead") to choose from the rest.

Steps 1 and 4 follow Google's strategy guide ([Plan a hybrid and multicloud strategy](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/hybrid-multicloud-patterns/strategy)). The SRE book calls absolute requirements, such as "always" available, unrealistic ([Service Level Objectives](https://sre.google/sre-book/service-level-objectives/)).

**Worked example.** A regional insurer sells policies online. Customers get a quote, buy a policy, and upload photos for claims. Traffic triples during storm season. Claims data must stay in the country. The company must close its data center in 12 months. Its six operations engineers have no container experience. The CIO wants 20% lower infrastructure cost and weekly releases.

| Statement | Type | Design consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Get a quote, buy a policy, upload photos | Functional | Defines the services, the APIs, and the data stores (see [Choosing storage](note:1.3-storage-choice)). |
| Traffic triples during storm season | Non-functional (scale) and a critical period | Plan capacity and test against peak load. Consider a production code freeze during the peak. |
| Claims data must stay in the country | Constraint (regulation) | Limits the regions for the data and for the DR copies. |
| Close the data center in 12 months | Constraint (time) | Favors rehost for workloads that can run as-is. Modernize them later. |
| No container experience | Constraint (skills) | Favors fully managed services and a training plan (see [Business processes](note:4.2-business-processes)). |
| 20% lower infrastructure cost | Success measure (KPI) | Needs a TCO baseline before the migration. |
| Weekly releases | Success measure (KPI) | Track DORA metrics and build CI/CD (see [SDLC and CI/CD](note:4.1-sdlc-cicd)). |

Sources: [Assess the reliability requirements](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/infra-reliability-guide/requirements), [Migrate to Google Cloud: Get started](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-getting-started), [Well-Architected Framework](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework), [Assess and discover your workloads](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads), [Align cloud spending with business value](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/cost-optimization/align-cloud-spending-business-value).

## Functional requirements, non-functional requirements, and constraints

| Category | Question it answers | Examples from Google docs | Where it changes the design |
|---|---|---|---|
| Functional | What must the system do? | Process orders. Write banking transactions to a ledger at the end of each day. | Components, APIs, and data model |
| Non-functional | How well must the system do it? | 5-nines availability for an ATM service. A data-freshness target of eight hours for the ledger batch. | Topology, redundancy, scaling, and SLOs |
| Constraint | What limits the choices? | Data residency. Quotas on compute cores to limit license cost. Hardware or operating systems that the public cloud does not offer. | Regions, machine types, and migration type |

Sources: [Assess the reliability requirements](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/infra-reliability-guide/requirements), [Plan a hybrid and multicloud strategy](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/hybrid-multicloud-patterns/strategy).

Use the Well-Architected Framework pillars as a checklist for non-functional requirements ([Well-Architected Framework](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework)). For the principles in each pillar, see [The Google Cloud Well-Architected Framework](note:1.2-well-architected-framework).

Four details decide many questions ([Assess the reliability requirements](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/infra-reliability-guide/requirements)):

- **Granularity.** One application can have several requirements. An order-processing application might need higher reliability for writes to the orders database than for reads.
- **Critical periods.** An application can be more critical at some times, often at peak load. An online trading platform might need 5-nines availability only during trading hours.
- **The measurement window.** After an outage, teams can find that they read "99.99%" differently. One team accepts a 45-minute outage once a year. Another team expects at most 4.3 minutes of downtime each month.
- **Change.** Requirements change as the business grows. Reassess them periodically and after an outage.

## Business use cases and product strategy

A cloud strategy exists to meet the business requirements of each business use case. Workload placement must follow business objectives and use cases with measurable business effects. Without an agreed vision, planning can lead to competing stakeholder visions. Write a vision statement that answers these questions ([Plan a hybrid and multicloud strategy](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/hybrid-multicloud-patterns/strategy)):

- What is the targeted business use case, and which business objectives does it meet?
- Why does the current approach and environment not meet the objectives?
- What are the primary technology aspects to optimize in the public cloud?
- Why and how does the new approach meet the objectives?
- How long do you plan to use the setup?

Product strategy often depends on speed: on-demand resources let an organization react faster to market changes and competitor actions ([What is cloud migration?](https://cloud.google.com/learn/cloud-migration)).

## Design decision trade-offs

| Trade-off | What Google's guidance says |
|---|---|
| Reliability and cost | Higher reliability can cost much more. If users are already happy, more reliability can give a low return on investment. |
| Recovery objectives and cost | Smaller RTO and RPO values make the application cost more to run. |
| Migration speed and cloud optimization | Rehost is the quickest type. Rehosted workloads do not take full advantage of horizontal scalability, fine-grained pricing, and managed services. |
| Downtime and migration cost | Zero and near-zero downtime migrations usually cost more and are more complex to build. |
| Data residency and global reach | Regulations can require data in specific countries, even when the business serves users globally. |
| Managed and self-managed | VMs can look cheaper, but patching and scaling raise the TCO. Serverless services lower operational overhead. |

Sources: [Set realistic targets for reliability](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/reliability/set-targets), [Disaster recovery planning guide](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/dr-scenarios-planning-guide), [Migrate to Google Cloud: Get started](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-getting-started), [Assess and discover your workloads](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads), [Assess the reliability requirements](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/infra-reliability-guide/requirements), [Align cloud spending with business value](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/cost-optimization/align-cloud-spending-business-value).

For trade-offs between pillars, see [The Google Cloud Well-Architected Framework](note:1.2-well-architected-framework).

Record each large trade-off in an architecture decision record (ADR). An ADR captures the options, the main requirements that drive the decision, and the decision. When you change a decision, keep the previous decision and the reason for the change ([Architecture decision records overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/architecture-decision-records)). For the decision process, see [Business processes](note:4.2-business-processes).

## Workload disposition: build, buy, modify, or deprecate

Map the business words of the exam guide to Google's migration types:

| Disposition | Google migration type | Use it when | Main cost or risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Move as-is | Rehost: lift and shift | The workload runs as-is in the target, or the business has little need for change. You cannot change the source code and cannot decommission the workload. | The workload does not take full advantage of cloud features such as horizontal scaling. |
| Modify (a little) | Replatform: lift and optimize | You want cloud capabilities, for example containers on GKE. | More work and more testing than rehost. |
| Modify (more) | Refactor: move and improve | The target does not support the current architecture, or a major update is due anyway. | Takes longer than rehost. Needs new skills. |
| Modify (most) | Re-architect: continue to modernize | You change how the code functions, for example a monolith becomes microservices. | More complex than refactor. Needs more testing. |
| Build | Rebuild: remove and replace | The app does not meet your goals, costs too much to migrate, or is not supported on Google Cloud. | Can take longer than rehost or refactor. Not suitable for ready-made apps. |
| Buy | Repurchase | A purchased on-premises workload has a SaaS equivalent, for example Google Workspace. | Might cost a lot more. Gives less granular control. |
| Deprecate | Retire | The application duplicates another application, for example after a merger. You decommission it. | Unfinished retirement plans leave "zombie applications" running. |
| Keep in place | Retain | The application stays in the current environment. | — |

Sources: [Migrate to Google Cloud: Get started](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-getting-started), [About migration planning](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/migration-planning-overview), [Application rationalization](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/application-development/application-rationalization-what-why-and-how).

Notes:

- Migration Center lists these paths: rehost, replatform, refactor, re-architect, replace, and retire ([About migration planning](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/migration-planning-overview)). The migration guide calls rebuild "remove and replace" ([Migrate to Google Cloud: Get started](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-getting-started)).
- Rehost is commonly a first step in a larger transformation ([What is cloud migration?](https://cloud.google.com/learn/cloud-migration)). See [Future improvements](note:1.5-future-improvements).
- Choose the type per workload. For waves and first movers, see [Planning a migration](note:1.4-migration-planning).

## Success measures: KPIs, ROI, and SLOs

| Measure | What it shows | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Business KPI | Value to the business | Revenue growth, cost savings, customer satisfaction, efficiency, adoption |
| Unit cost | Whether more spend comes from profitable growth | Revenue per customer, transaction volume |
| Delivery KPI | Speed and stability of change | DORA metrics: change failure rate, time to detect, time to restore |
| SLI | The service level that users see | Success ratio of user requests, latency, error rate |
| SLO | The target value for an SLI | Average search latency less than 100 milliseconds |
| SLA | A contract with consequences | A rebate or a penalty when an SLO is missed |
| Migration success criteria | Whether a wave or a proof of concept succeeded | Workload compatibility, migration downtime, performance targets |

Sources: [AI and ML perspective: Cost optimization](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/perspectives/ai-ml/cost-optimization), [Align cloud spending with business value](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/cost-optimization/align-cloud-spending-business-value), [Define reliability based on user-experience goals](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/reliability/define-reliability-based-on-user-experience-goals), [Service Level Objectives](https://sre.google/sre-book/service-level-objectives/), [Best practices for validating a migration plan](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-google-cloud-best-practices).

How to use the measures:

- Write objectives as SMART goals: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound ([Continuously monitor and improve performance](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/performance-optimization/continuously-monitor-and-improve-performance)).
- To measure ROI, define KPIs for costs (including unit costs) and for business value ([AI and ML perspective: Cost optimization](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/perspectives/ai-ml/cost-optimization)).
- Measure before you move. The assess phase includes TCO calculations and app performance benchmarks ([Migrate to Google Cloud: Get started](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-getting-started)).
- SLO targets are a business decision too. Choosing targets is not a purely technical activity, because of the product and business implications ([Service Level Objectives](https://sre.google/sre-book/service-level-objectives/)).
- An RTO and an RPO are measurable, so treat them as SLOs ([Disaster recovery planning guide](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/dr-scenarios-planning-guide)).

## Security, compliance, and observability as requirements

- **Security.** Add cloud and network security from the initial design phase ([Security, privacy, and compliance pillar](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/security)). See [IAM and resource hierarchy](note:3.1-iam-and-hierarchy).
- **Compliance.** Some data must stay in a specific region. Some licenses forbid cloud deployment, and some sensitive workloads need sole tenancy ([Assess and discover your workloads](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)). See [Designing for compliance](note:3.2-compliance).
- **Observability.** Plan metrics, logs, and traces. Track technical metrics that can serve as SLIs, and business metrics such as orders placed and payments received ([Detect potential failures by using observability](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/reliability/observability)). See [Google Cloud Observability](note:6.2-observability).

## Cost optimization as a business requirement

Cost has two sides: the price of resources and the cost to manage them. Maximize business value and minimize TCO ([Align cloud spending with business value](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/cost-optimization/align-cloud-spending-business-value)). A migration business case compares the Google Cloud TCO with the full cost of the current data center ([Assess and discover your workloads](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)). For pricing levers, budgets, and CapEx and OpEx, see [Cost optimization](note:4.2-cost-optimization). For license impact, see [Licensing and financials](note:1.4-licensing-and-financials).

## Business continuity plan

DR is a subset of business continuity planning ([Disaster recovery planning guide](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/dr-scenarios-planning-guide)).

- A business impact analysis defines the RTO and the RPO for each application ([Disaster recovery planning guide](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/dr-scenarios-planning-guide)).
- Match the DR pattern to the recovery goals. Historical compliance data can use a cold pattern. An online service fits a hot pattern. An email notification system is a candidate for a warm pattern ([Disaster recovery planning guide](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/dr-scenarios-planning-guide)).
- Plan capacity for critical periods, and test the application against peak load ([Assess the reliability requirements](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/infra-reliability-guide/requirements)).

For DR patterns and tests, see [Disaster recovery planning](note:4.1-disaster-recovery). For zone and region failure design, see [HA and scalability](note:1.2-ha-scalability-performance).

## Exam traps

- **More availability is not always better.** When users are satisfied and an outage has low business impact, a more expensive design gives a low return. Choose "just reliable enough" ([Set realistic targets for reliability](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/reliability/set-targets)).
- **SLA and SLO are not the same.** An SLA has explicit consequences, such as a rebate or a penalty. An internal target without consequences is an SLO ([Service Level Objectives](https://sre.google/sre-book/service-level-objectives/)).
- **Server metrics as success measures.** CPU usage can be high while users see no problem. Use user-facing SLIs such as the request success ratio ([Define reliability based on user-experience goals](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/reliability/define-reliability-based-on-user-experience-goals)).
- **Rebuild under a tight deadline.** When the deadline is short or the source code cannot change, the answer is usually rehost, not refactor or rebuild ([Migrate to Google Cloud: Get started](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-getting-started)).
- **"Buy" is always cheaper.** Repurchase can need fewer resources, but it can cost more and gives less control ([Migrate to Google Cloud: Get started](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-getting-started)).
- **One DR tier for all applications.** Set the RTO and the RPO for each application from a business impact analysis. Smaller values cost more ([Disaster recovery planning guide](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/dr-scenarios-planning-guide)).
- **A TCO that compares only server prices.** Include power, cooling, maintenance, and support in the current costs, and network costs in the cloud costs ([Assess and discover your workloads](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)).
- **Zero downtime for every workload.** Zero and near-zero downtime migrations cost more. Use them only for workloads that cannot tolerate downtime ([Assess and discover your workloads](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)).
