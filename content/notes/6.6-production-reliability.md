## Why it matters

Objective 6.6 asks how you prove that a solution stays reliable in production. The exam names three methods: chaos engineering, penetration testing, and load testing. A typical question gives a constraint, such as "prove that the app survives a zone failure" or "traffic triples on launch day". Wrong answers often ask Google for permission, confuse quota with capacity, or retry with no backoff.

## Key points

- The error budget is one minus the availability target. SRE teams spend it on launches and experiments, and they keep operational work (toil) below 50% of their time ([Introduction (SRE book)](https://sre.google/sre-book/introduction/), [Eliminating Toil (SRE book)](https://sre.google/sre-book/eliminating-toil/)).
- Test recovery periodically, including regional failovers, release rollbacks, and data restores. Prefer a staging environment. In production, prepare automated monitoring and manual rollback procedures ([Perform testing for recovery from failures](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/reliability/perform-testing-for-recovery-from-failures)).
- Fault Injection Testing (Preview) fails Compute Engine and GKE resources, degrades traffic through a layer 7 load balancer, and fails over Cloud SQL. Its faults stay inside one project and one region ([Choose setup instructions for a fault type](https://docs.cloud.google.com/fault-injection-testing/set-up-experiment)).
- You do not need to contact Google before a penetration test of your projects. Follow the Acceptable Use Policy and the Terms of Service, and affect only your own projects ([Cloud Security FAQ](https://support.google.com/cloud/answer/6262505?hl=en)).
- Web Security Scanner scans public App Engine, GKE, and Compute Engine web apps. Custom scans can change data, so run them in a test environment with a test account ([Overview of Web Security Scanner](https://docs.cloud.google.com/security-command-center/docs/concepts-web-security-scanner-overview)).
- In a load test, control the rate and the concurrency, confirm that the service scales out linearly, and measure performance at the client ([Load testing best practices](https://docs.cloud.google.com/run/docs/about-load-testing)).
- Quotas do not guarantee that resources are available. A reservation gives a high assurance of zonal capacity, and you pay the on-demand rate while it exists ([Allocation quotas](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/resource-usage), [About reservations](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/docs/instances/reservations-overview)).
- For a regional MIG in three zones, Google recommends overprovisioning to at least 150%. Then two zones can carry all the traffic ([About regional MIGs](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/docs/instance-groups/regional-migs)).
- Retry with randomized exponential backoff, limit the retries for each request, and retry at one layer only ([Addressing Cascading Failures (SRE book)](https://sre.google/sre-book/addressing-cascading-failures/)).

## SRE principles for production

Site reliability engineering (SRE) is Google's approach to operations. The SRE book lists tenets that shape the correct answers for this objective.

| SRE tenet | What it means | Where this guide covers it |
|---|---|---|
| Error budgets | The business sets the availability target. The error budget is one minus that target, and the team spends it to launch features faster. | [Quality control measures](note:6.5-quality-control) |
| A durable focus on engineering | Operational work stays below 50% of each SRE's time. The rest goes to engineering that reduces future toil or adds service features. | This page |
| Monitoring | Software interprets the signals. Humans get an alert only when they must act now, and a ticket when they must act in days. | [Google Cloud Observability](note:6.2-observability) |
| Emergency response | Reliability depends on mean time to repair (MTTR). A written playbook gives about 3 times better MTTR than improvisation. | [Supporting deployed solutions](note:6.4-support) |
| Change management | About 70% of outages come from changes. Use progressive rollouts, fast problem detection, and safe rollbacks. | [Deployment and release management](note:6.3-release-management) |
| Demand forecasting and capacity planning | Forecast organic and inorganic demand, and load test regularly. | This page |

Sources: [Introduction (SRE book)](https://sre.google/sre-book/introduction/), [Eliminating Toil (SRE book)](https://sre.google/sre-book/eliminating-toil/).

Toil is work tied to a production service that is manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical, without enduring value, and that grows linearly with the service ([Eliminating Toil (SRE book)](https://sre.google/sre-book/eliminating-toil/)). A failover test that an engineer runs by hand each quarter is toil. The Well-Architected Framework recommends that you automate failover tests in your CI/CD pipelines ([Perform testing for recovery from failures](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/reliability/perform-testing-for-recovery-from-failures)).

## Chaos engineering and failure drills

Chaos engineering injects failures on purpose, so that you find weak points before your users find them. The reliability pillar of the Well-Architected Framework describes the method as a cycle ([Perform testing for recovery from failures](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/reliability/perform-testing-for-recovery-from-failures)):

1. Define the objectives and the scope, for example to validate the RTO and the RPO.
2. Prepare the environment. Prefer staging, back up critical data, and inform the stakeholders.
3. Simulate the failures with tools such as Chaos Monkey or custom scripts, and escalate gradually. Run load tests at the same time. Include misconfigurations, such as wrong DNS failover settings.
4. Monitor how load balancers and health checks move traffic. Measure latency, errors, and throughput, and confirm that the alerts fire.
5. Verify the recovery time against the RTO and the data integrity against the RPO.
6. Document the results, present a postmortem, and update the playbooks.
7. Repeat on a schedule, for example quarterly, and automate the tests in CI/CD.

### How to inject each failure

| Failure to simulate | Google Cloud method | What to know |
|---|---|---|
| A VM crashes | Stop or delete a VM of a MIG with `gcloud compute instances` commands | The MIG repairs a VM that an action outside the MIG stops or deletes. `gcloud compute instance-groups managed delete-instances` reduces the target size instead, so no repair happens. |
| A GKE zone fails | Remove the zone from the node pool, use a single-zone node pool, or cordon and drain the nodes of one zone | These methods affect only the nodes. The GKE control plane is not affected. |
| Compute or Cloud SQL failure, managed | Fault Injection Testing (Preview): Fail Compute, Failover Cloud SQL | During Preview, you request access through your Google Cloud account manager. |
| HTTP errors or extra latency | `faultInjectionPolicy` in a URL map route, or in a Cloud Service Mesh route | Delays or aborts a percentage of requests. Fault Injection Testing also has a Degrade Application Traffic fault. |
| A region loses its network | Scripts that apply firewall rules or API restrictions | The Well-Architected Framework suggests this for a region-wide outage test. |

Sources: [About repairing VMs for high availability](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/docs/instance-groups/about-repair), [Add and remove VMs from a MIG](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/docs/instance-groups/add-remove-vms-in-mig), [Simulate a zone failure in GKE regional clusters](https://docs.cloud.google.com/kubernetes-engine/docs/tutorials/simulate-zone-failure), [Choose setup instructions for a fault type](https://docs.cloud.google.com/fault-injection-testing/set-up-experiment), [Fault Injection Testing release notes](https://docs.cloud.google.com/fault-injection-testing/release-notes), [Set up traffic management for global external Application Load Balancers](https://docs.cloud.google.com/load-balancing/docs/https/setting-up-global-traffic-mgmt), [Advanced traffic management overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/service-mesh/docs/service-routing/advanced-traffic-management), [Perform testing for recovery from failures](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/reliability/perform-testing-for-recovery-from-failures).

Before you use Fault Injection Testing, make sure that the target environment has some redundancy. Give permission to run experiments only to operators who understand the architecture ([Fault Injection Testing overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/fault-injection-testing/overview)). Redundancy also needs spare capacity. After a zone fails, a regional MIG adds no VMs in the other zones unless you configure autoscaling ([About regional MIGs](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/docs/instance-groups/regional-migs)). For DR drills, see [Disaster recovery planning](note:4.1-disaster-recovery). For multi-zone designs, see [High availability, scalability, and performance](note:1.2-ha-scalability-performance).

## Penetration testing and vulnerability scanning

You can penetration test your own Google Cloud projects without notice to Google. You must follow the Acceptable Use Policy and the Terms of Service, and your tests must affect only your projects. Report a vulnerability in Google Cloud through the Vulnerability Reward Program ([Cloud Security FAQ](https://support.google.com/cloud/answer/6262505?hl=en)). The Acceptable Use Policy forbids use of the services to disrupt them, or to test them for limitations or vulnerabilities, except as your agreement permits ([Google Cloud Acceptable Use Policy](https://cloud.google.com/terms/aup)). Test your applications, not the Google infrastructure below them.

Web Security Scanner, a Security Command Center service, finds vulnerabilities such as cross-site scripting, outdated libraries, and mixed content in web apps. Its findings map to categories in the OWASP Top Ten ([Overview of Web Security Scanner](https://docs.cloud.google.com/security-command-center/docs/concepts-web-security-scanner-overview)).

| | Managed scans | Custom scans |
|---|---|---|
| Security Command Center tier | Premium (and the deprecated Enterprise tier) | Standard-legacy, Premium, and the deprecated Enterprise tier |
| Setup | Security Command Center finds and scans public endpoints once each week | You define each scan at the project level |
| Behavior | No authentication and `GET` requests only, so no forms are submitted | Activates every input and control that it finds, so it can change data |
| Ports | Default ports only (80 and 443) | Use a custom scan for other ports |

Sources: [Overview of Web Security Scanner](https://docs.cloud.google.com/security-command-center/docs/concepts-web-security-scanner-overview).

More facts from the same page:

- The scanner supports only public URLs and IP addresses that are not behind a firewall.
- It does not replace a manual security review.
- To scan safely, use a test project and a test account, the `inq-no-click` CSS class on dangerous controls, excluded URLs, and a data backup.

For the wider security posture, see [Security controls](note:3.1-security-controls).

## Load testing

Load testing simulates realistic traffic patterns. Stress testing pushes the system to its limits to find bottlenecks ([Ensure operational readiness and performance using CloudOps](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/operational-excellence/operational-readiness-and-performance-using-cloudops)). The SRE book adds three rules ([Addressing Cascading Failures (SRE book)](https://sre.google/sre-book/addressing-cascading-failures/)):

- Load test components until they break. A good component rejects some requests and survives. A weak component crashes or serves a very high rate of errors.
- Test gradual and sudden load increases, because caches make the results different.
- Test the return to normal load. Check that a component leaves its degraded mode without human action.

The most concrete Google guidance is for Cloud Run ([Load testing best practices](https://docs.cloud.google.com/run/docs/about-load-testing)):

- Use a test harness, such as JMeter, that controls the rate and the concurrency. Pub/Sub is a poor load generator, because you cannot control its rate.
- Set minimum instances to at least 1 to avoid spurious cold starts.
- For more than the default maximum of 100 instances, set a higher maximum and work with your account team.
- Export logs to BigQuery for a second-by-second analysis. Monitoring data is not fine-grained enough.
- Repeat the test at different loads to confirm linear scale-out, and check performance as the client experiences it.

What a Cloud Run service does at its limit:

| Situation | Behavior | What it means for the test |
|---|---|---|
| All instances are busy and the service is at maximum instances | Requests wait for up to 10 seconds, or 3.5 times the average startup time if that is longer. Then they fail with HTTP `429`. | The client sees queue latency and `429` errors. |
| Server metrics during that overload | `request_count` excludes requests that do not reach an instance. `request_latencies` starts when a request reaches the container. | Server charts can look healthy. Trust client-side results. |

Sources: [About instance autoscaling in Cloud Run services](https://docs.cloud.google.com/run/docs/about-instance-autoscaling), [Google Cloud metrics: P through Z](https://docs.cloud.google.com/monitoring/api/metrics_gcp_p_z).

## Capacity planning

The SRE book requires a demand forecast beyond the lead time for capacity, with inorganic demand such as launches, and regular load tests ([Introduction (SRE book)](https://sre.google/sre-book/introduction/)). The Well-Architected Framework adds quota management, trend analysis, seasonal peaks, and enough DR capacity for a graceful failover ([Ensure operational readiness and performance using CloudOps](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/operational-excellence/operational-readiness-and-performance-using-cloudops)).

| Need | Tool | Key fact |
|---|---|---|
| Permission to create more resources | Quotas, quota alerts, and the quota adjuster | Quotas do not guarantee that resources are available in a region or zone. |
| Assured capacity now | On-demand reservation | Zonal. You pay the on-demand rate for the reserved resources while the reservation exists. |
| Assured capacity at a later date | Future reservation | Use it when you do not need the capacity right away. |
| Survive the loss of a zone | Regional MIG with spare VMs | At least 150% of the needed VMs in three zones, or 200% in two zones. |
| Protect a backing service | Cloud Run maximum instances | Limits the connections to a database with a fixed connection limit. |

Sources: [Allocation quotas](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/resource-usage), [Cloud Quotas overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/docs/quotas/overview), [About reservations](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/docs/instances/reservations-overview), [Choose a reservation type](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/docs/instances/choose-reservation-type), [About regional MIGs](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/docs/instance-groups/regional-migs), [Set maximum instances for services](https://docs.cloud.google.com/run/docs/configuring/max-instances).

## Graceful degradation and overload protection

Graceful degradation keeps a system working under high load, possibly with lower performance or accuracy. The Well-Architected Framework recommends five practices ([Design for graceful degradation](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/reliability/graceful-degradation)):

1. Throttle requests in each replica, for example with Apigee.
2. Drop excess requests early, at the frontend. Some users see errors, but the backends survive. A circuit breaker drops all traffic instead.
3. Handle partial errors and retries in the application.
4. Test overload scenarios regularly.
5. Monitor traffic spikes, so that you act before an overload.

The SRE book separates two ideas. Load shedding drops a part of the traffic, for example with HTTP 503 when too many requests are in flight. Graceful degradation reduces the work for each request, for example with a search of cached data only. Exercise degraded modes regularly, because a code path that you never use often does not work ([Addressing Cascading Failures (SRE book)](https://sre.google/sre-book/addressing-cascading-failures/)).

### Retries

Retries can turn a small overload into a cascading failure. The SRE book gives these rules ([Addressing Cascading Failures (SRE book)](https://sre.google/sre-book/addressing-cascading-failures/)):

- Always use randomized exponential backoff. Without random jitter, retries from many clients arrive at the same time.
- Limit the retries for each request. Consider a retry budget for each server process, for example 60 retries each minute.
- Retry at one layer only. Four attempts at each of three layers create 64 attempts on the database.
- Do not retry permanent errors. Return a clear overload status, so that clients back off.

A Google example algorithm waits `min(2^n + random_number_milliseconds, maximum_backoff)` seconds before retry `n`. The random part is up to 1,000 ms, and `maximum_backoff` is typically 32 or 64 seconds ([Exponential backoff](https://docs.cloud.google.com/memorystore/docs/redis/exponential-backoff)). Retry only transient responses (HTTP 408, 429, and 5xx, or a timeout), and check that the request is idempotent ([Retry strategy](https://docs.cloud.google.com/storage/docs/retry-strategy)).

### Google Cloud features for overload protection

| Mechanism | Feature | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| Limit each client | Cloud Armor throttle and rate-based ban rules | Cloud Armor applies the threshold to each backend service. |
| Retry at the proxy | Global external Application Load Balancer | Without a retry policy, it retries a failed `GET` once for HTTP 502, 503, or 504. A `retryPolicy` in the URL map changes this. |
| Remove bad endpoints | `outlierDetection` on the backend service | Supported for the global external Application Load Balancer and Cloud Service Mesh. |
| Limit connections and requests | `circuitBreakers` on the backend service | Supported for internal Application Load Balancers and Cloud Service Mesh. |

Sources: [Rate limiting overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/armor/docs/rate-limiting-overview), [Request distribution for external Application Load Balancers](https://docs.cloud.google.com/load-balancing/docs/https/request-distribution), [Traffic management overview for global external Application Load Balancers](https://docs.cloud.google.com/load-balancing/docs/https/traffic-management-global), [Traffic management overview for internal Application Load Balancers](https://docs.cloud.google.com/load-balancing/docs/l7-internal/traffic-management), [Advanced traffic management overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/service-mesh/docs/service-routing/advanced-traffic-management).

## Exam traps

- **Ask Google for approval before a penetration test.** You do not need to contact Google. Follow the Acceptable Use Policy and the Terms of Service, and make sure that your tests affect only your projects ([Cloud Security FAQ](https://support.google.com/cloud/answer/6262505?hl=en)).
- **Custom Web Security Scanner scans on production with an administrator account.** The scanner can post comments or create many test emails. Use a test environment and a test account ([Overview of Web Security Scanner](https://docs.cloud.google.com/security-command-center/docs/concepts-web-security-scanner-overview)).
- **A quota increase to guarantee DR capacity.** Quotas do not guarantee resources. Use reservations ([Allocation quotas](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/resource-usage), [About reservations](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/docs/instances/reservations-overview)).
- **Three zones with no spare VMs.** After a zone fails, the MIG adds no VMs unless you autoscale. Overprovision to at least 150% ([About regional MIGs](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/docs/instance-groups/regional-migs)).
- **Immediate retries at every layer.** Use randomized exponential backoff and a retry limit. Retry at one layer only, because the attempts multiply from layer to layer ([Addressing Cascading Failures (SRE book)](https://sre.google/sre-book/addressing-cascading-failures/)).
- **A circuit breaker as the first overload defense.** Google recommends that you drop excess requests early. A circuit breaker drops all traffic ([Design for graceful degradation](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/reliability/graceful-degradation)).
- **Pub/Sub as the load generator.** You cannot control its rate or concurrency ([Load testing best practices](https://docs.cloud.google.com/run/docs/about-load-testing)).
- **Server-side charts as proof of a good Cloud Run load test.** `request_count` excludes requests that fail at the maximum instances limit ([Google Cloud metrics: P through Z](https://docs.cloud.google.com/monitoring/api/metrics_gcp_p_z)).
- **`delete-instances` to test MIG repair.** It reduces the target size, so the MIG does not recreate the VM ([Add and remove VMs from a MIG](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/docs/instance-groups/add-remove-vms-in-mig)).
