## Why it matters

A migration plan decides what moves, in which order, with which migration type, and how you go back when a step fails. The exam describes a company with existing systems and asks for the first step, the best first workloads, or the right cutover approach. Most wrong answers skip a phase: they move workloads before the assessment, before the landing zone, or without a rollback plan.

## Key points

- Google describes four migration phases: assess, plan, deploy, and optimize ([Migration: Get started](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-getting-started)).
- Google names six migration types: rehost, replatform, refactor, re-architect, rebuild, and repurchase ([Migration: Get started](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-getting-started)).
- The inventory includes dependencies, supporting services, servers, and appliances. It also records licensing terms and data location restrictions ([Assess and discover](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)).
- Google Cloud Migration Center discovers assets, groups them, and generates total cost of ownership (TCO) reports from your migration preferences ([Discovery and assessment](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/discovery-and-assessment-overview)).
- A landing zone is often a prerequisite to deploying enterprise workloads ([Landing zone design](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/landing-zones)).
- Good first movers are not business critical, have few dependencies, need little refactoring, and can afford a cutover window ([Assess and discover](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)).
- Group workloads into waves by business value and effort. Wave 1 has high value and low effort ([Plan your migration waves](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/plan-migration-waves)).
- Prepare a rollback strategy for each step, and set a maximum execution time for each step ([Validating a migration plan](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-google-cloud-best-practices)).
- Zero-downtime migrations are usually more costly and complex ([Assess and discover](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)). Avoid a zero-downtime requirement where possible ([Validating a migration plan](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-google-cloud-best-practices)).
- Google recommends a technical design document, a RACI matrix, and a timeline such as a T-Minus plan ([Assess and discover](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)).
- Define criteria to retire the source environment, so that you do not pay for two environments indefinitely ([Validating a migration plan](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-google-cloud-best-practices)).

## The four phases

The phases repeat inside each migration wave, because discovery and assessment continue through the whole project ([Execute your migration](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/migration-execution)).

| Phase | What you do | Typical outputs |
|---|---|---|
| Assess | Build the inventory, find dependencies, calculate TCO, and set performance baselines. | Inventory, workload catalog, TCO report |
| Plan | Build the foundation (identity, resource hierarchy, networking) and prioritize the workloads. | Landing zone, wave plan, plan documents |
| Deploy | Design and run the process that moves the workloads. | Migrated workloads, test results |
| Optimize | Use cloud capabilities for performance, scale, disaster recovery, and cost. | Lessons learned for the next waves |

Sources: [Migration: Get started](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-getting-started), [Execute your migration](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/migration-execution).

## Choose a migration type for each workload

Choose the type per workload. Successful migrations change the workload as little as possible during the move ([Migration: Get started](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-getting-started)).

| Type (Google's term) | Use it when | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Rehost: lift and shift | The workload runs as-is on Google Cloud, or you cannot change its code or build. | Fastest. Teams keep their tools and skills, but the workload does not take full advantage of cloud features such as horizontal scaling. |
| Replatform: lift and optimize | You want cloud capabilities, for example containers on GKE. | More work than rehost, and several rounds of testing. |
| Refactor: move and improve | The current architecture is not supported as-is, or a major update is due anyway. | Takes longer than rehost. Teams must learn new skills. |
| Re-architect: continue to modernize | You want to change how the code functions, for example split a monolith into microservices. | More complex than refactor. It can add bugs, so it needs several rounds of testing. |
| Rebuild: remove and replace ("rip and replace") | The workload does not meet your goals, costs too much to migrate, or is not supported on Google Cloud. | Can take longer than rehost or refactor. Not suitable for ready-made apps, because you rewrite the app. |
| Repurchase | A SaaS product can replace a purchased product, for example Google Workspace. | Less effort, but it can cost more and gives less control. |

Source: [Migration: Get started](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-getting-started).

Two rules decide many questions:

- If you cannot refactor a workload and cannot decommission it, you must rehost it ([Migration: Get started](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-getting-started)).
- First movers need only the changes that let them run in the target environment. Modernization comes in later phases ([Assess and discover](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)).

Objective 1.1 calls this decision "workload disposition" ([Business requirements](note:1.1-business-requirements)). The tools for each type are in [Migration tooling](note:5.1-migration-tooling).

## Assess: inventory and workload catalog

Manual inventories become outdated quickly. For a large or changing environment, automate the inventory. Collect at least these items ([Assess and discover](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)):

- Each workload and its dependencies, such as databases and message brokers.
- Supporting services, such as source repositories, CI/CD tools, and artifact repositories.
- Servers, runtime environments, and physical appliances such as firewalls.
- For each item: whether you can change the source code, the deployment method, network restrictions, IP address requirements, licensing, and the authentication method.

Also assess how teams build, store, configure, and monitor the workloads ([Assess and discover](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)).

Then build a workload catalog. A catalog matrix has one dimension per criterion, for example business criticality, dependencies, maximum allowable downtime, and migration difficulty. Give each workload a migration complexity indicator, such as "easy", "hard", or "cannot be migrated" ([Assess and discover](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)). Use a proof of concept (PoC) for each workload category ([Assess and discover](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)). A PoC can measure the downtime and validate the rollback before the full migration ([Validating a migration plan](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-google-cloud-best-practices)).

## Google Cloud Migration Center

Migration Center is a unified platform for cost estimates, asset discovery, assessment, and planning ([Migration Center overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/migration-center-overview)). You activate it once per project. You select a region for Migration Center data, and you cannot change it later. Cost estimates and assessments have no extra cost ([Get started with Migration Center](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/get-started-with-migration-center)).

| Discovery method | Tool | Use it when |
|---|---|---|
| Automated one-time collection | Migration Center discovery client CLI (`mcdc`) | You need a quick snapshot of VMs, physical servers, AWS assets, or databases. |
| Automated continuous collection | Discovery client, installed on a Windows machine | You want configuration and performance data over several days. |
| Manual import | CSV templates (`vmInfo`, `perfInfo`, `diskInfo`, `tagInfo`), RVTools exports, or scripts for other clouds | You cannot install a collector, or you have few assets. |

Sources: [Start an asset discovery](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/start-asset-discovery), [Discovery client overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/discovery-client-overview), [Upload data tables](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/import-data-tables).

The discovery client is agentless. It does not scan AIX or Solaris ([Discovery client overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/discovery-client-overview)). For VMware, you can upload RVTools data (version 4.3.1 or later) instead ([Import RVTools data](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/import-data-rvtools)). Other than IP addresses, Migration Center does not collect personally identifiable information ([Start an asset discovery](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/start-asset-discovery)).

After discovery, you group assets, create migration preferences, and generate a TCO report ([Discovery and assessment](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/discovery-and-assessment-overview)).

| Feature | What it does |
|---|---|
| Groups | A set of assets, for example the servers and databases of one application. An asset can be in several groups. |
| Migration preferences | Region, target product (Compute Engine or VMware Engine), machine series, tenancy, OS licensing, pricing track, and sizing strategy. |
| TCO report | Compares up to four preference sets for each of up to five groups. |
| Other reports | Detailed pricing, assets inventory, assets performance, network dependencies, and a license report for Microsoft software. |
| Network dependency visualization (Preview) | A graph of connections between server assets. |
| Rapid cost estimate (Preview) | An offline estimate over a 5-year horizon. It does not need activation. |

Sources: [Group assets](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/create-groups), [Create and manage migration preferences](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/migration-preferences), [Migration preferences for servers](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/server-preferences), [Generate TCO reports](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/generate-tco-report), [Available reports](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/migration-center-reports), [Visualize network dependencies](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/visualize-network-dependencies), [Cost estimation overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/estimate/overview), [Migration Center overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/migration-center-overview), [Get started with Migration Center](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/get-started-with-migration-center).

Rightsizing depends on performance data. For an asset without performance data, Migration Center uses the target sizing strategy. The TCO report shows how many assets it sized that way ([Generate TCO reports](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/generate-tco-report)). Generate reports for different load profiles, such as peak and off-peak times ([Validating a migration plan](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-google-cloud-best-practices)). Licensing and TCO detail is in [Licensing and financials](note:1.4-licensing-and-financials).

## Plan: build the foundation first

The foundation covers identity, resource hierarchy, IAM, billing, connectivity, security, monitoring, and governance. A poorly planned foundation can cause delays, confusion, and downtime ([Build your foundation](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-google-cloud-building-your-foundation)). To deploy a landing zone, you must first create an organization resource and a billing account ([Landing zone design](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/landing-zones)).

Prebuilt starting points are the Google Cloud setup checklist and the enterprise foundations blueprint ([Build your foundation](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-google-cloud-building-your-foundation)). Teams that use Terraform can also start from Terraform blueprints and modules or Cloud Foundation Fabric ([Assess and discover](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)). See [IAM and resource hierarchy](note:3.1-iam-and-hierarchy), [Network design](note:1.3-network-design), and [Infrastructure as Code](note:5.2-infrastructure-as-code).

## Network and dependency planning

The network is also a migration tool: load balancers and DNS servers can direct traffic according to the plan ([Validating a migration plan](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-google-cloud-best-practices)).

| Topic | What to plan |
|---|---|
| Connectivity | Cloud Interconnect has a production-level SLA only in specific topologies. |
| MTU | Adjust the MTU for protocol overhead. Cloud VPN can need a lower MTU. |
| DNS | Make DNS records resolve in both environments. Cloud DNS can forward queries to your existing DNS servers. |
| Routes | Make sure that routes propagate correctly between the environments. |
| IP addresses | Record IP address requirements. To keep your public IPv4 addresses, use Bring your own IP. |
| Data transfer cost | You pay for data transfer instead of a fixed hardware cost. Egress and cross-region traffic are charged. |
| Backup access | If you set up Partner Interconnect during the migration, also set up backup access through the internet. |

Sources: [Build your foundation](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-google-cloud-building-your-foundation), [Validating a migration plan](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-google-cloud-best-practices).

Bandwidth decides the data transfer plan. At 1 Gbps, an online transfer of 100 TB takes over 10 days. At 100 Mbps, it takes over 100 days ([Transfer your large datasets](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-google-cloud-transferring-your-large-datasets)). See [Hybrid and multicloud connectivity](note:2.1-hybrid-multicloud) and [Migration tooling](note:5.1-migration-tooling).

For dependencies:

- Draw a dependency graph from the inventory ([Assess and discover](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)).
- Use the Migration Center network dependencies report. It lists the IP address, port, protocol, and service of each connection ([Available reports](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/migration-center-reports)).
- Put resources that must move together into one move group ([Execute your migration](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/migration-execution)).
- Traffic between migrated and non-migrated workloads can affect SLAs. Size the interconnect for it ([Migration risks](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/migration-risks)).

## Waves and first movers

| Wave | Business value | Effort | Guidance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | High | Low | Early migrations or proofs of concept |
| 2 | High | High | Can come next |
| 3 | Low | Low | Can come next |
| 4 | Low | High | Migrate last |

Source: [Plan your migration waves](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/plan-migration-waves).

The first movers let the team learn with low risk. Use these criteria ([Assess and discover](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)):

- **Not business critical, but used.** A rarely used workload does not build momentum, and problems are hard to detect.
- **Not an edge case.** It shows the common patterns of your company.
- **A motivated team** with a leadership sponsor.
- **Few dependencies,** loosely coupled, and preferably stateless.
- **Little refactoring,** ideally configuration changes only.
- **Few licensing and compliance restrictions.**
- **Can afford the downtime** of a cutover window.

Increase the batch size as the team gains experience ([Assess and discover](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)). Assess the risks of each wave. For example, move workloads with technical blockers to later waves, and put regulated workloads in separate waves ([Migration risks](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/migration-risks)).

## Cutover, rollback, and retirement

Choose the data migration approach from the data size, the change rate, the allowed downtime, and the consistency model ([Transfer your large datasets](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-google-cloud-transferring-your-large-datasets)).

| Approach | How it works | Cutover window | Main effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scheduled maintenance (big bang) | Copy the data, stop the writers, copy the changes, then switch. | Longest | Operations |
| Continuous replication (online or trickle) | Copy the data, then replicate changes until you switch. | Short | Operations |
| Y (writing and reading) | Workloads write to both sites. Reads move to the new site, then old writes stop. | None | Development: refactor every workload |
| Data-access microservice | One service owns all reads and writes and moves them to the new site. | None | Development: refactor one component |

Source: [Transfer your large datasets](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-google-cloud-transferring-your-large-datasets).

For databases, truly zero downtime is impossible, but near-zero downtime is possible ([Database migration, Part 1](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/database-migration-concepts-principles-part-1)). The phases are initial load, continuing migration, draining, switchover, and source deletion. A fallback is a reverse migration, so treat it as a full migration with tests ([Database migration, Part 2](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/database-migration-concepts-principles-part-2)).

Cutover and rollback practices ([Validating a migration plan](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-google-cloud-best-practices)):

1. Prepare a rollback strategy for each step, and test it periodically.
2. Set a maximum execution time for each step. When the time expires, start the rollback.
3. Set the TTL of the DNS records to change to its minimum before the cutover.
4. Check that clients respect the TTL. Some applications cache DNS entries.

After a failed deployment, run the rollback plan, run application tests, and roll back external changes, such as upstream configuration ([Execute your migration](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/migration-execution)).

Retire the source environment only when the criteria that you define are met. Examples of criteria ([Validating a migration plan](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-google-cloud-best-practices)):

- All workloads, with their backups and HA and DR mechanisms, run in the target environment.
- The migrated data is consistent, accessible, and complete.
- Performance meets your SLA targets.
- No traffic that belongs to the target environment still goes to the source environment.
- The workloads ran without issues for a period that you define.

## Integrating with existing systems during the hybrid period

- **Move frontends first.** In the tiered hybrid pattern, frontends move to Google Cloud and backends stay in the private environment. Frontends are often stateless, so they are easier to move. They reach the backends, ideally through an API gateway ([Tiered hybrid pattern](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/hybrid-multicloud-patterns-and-practices/tiered-hybrid-pattern)).
- **Make artifacts available in both environments,** so that you do not rebuild the artifact process during the migration ([Assess and discover](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)).
- **Monitor both environments.** Adjust the source alerts as you turn down source servers ([Validating a migration plan](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-google-cloud-best-practices)).

More patterns are in [Integration patterns and data movement](note:1.1-integration-and-data-movement).

## Testing the migration

- Define PoC success criteria, such as compatibility, downtime, and performance ([Validating a migration plan](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-google-cloud-best-practices)).
- Simulate the failure modes of each workload in a non-critical environment ([Validating a migration plan](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-google-cloud-best-practices)).
- Before a large data transfer, run a performance test on 3–5% of the data ([Transfer your large datasets](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-google-cloud-transferring-your-large-datasets)).
- Before you open a migrated database to clients, test basic functions and back it up ([Database migration, Part 2](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/database-migration-concepts-principles-part-2)).

Software test strategy is in [Testing and troubleshooting](note:4.1-testing-and-troubleshooting).

## Exam traps

- **The most important system first.** A mistake in a business-critical first mover hits the main business ([Assess and discover](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)).
- **The least used system first.** It is low risk, but it does not build momentum, and problems are hard to detect ([Assess and discover](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)).
- **Workloads before the foundation.** Assess first, and build the landing zone before the first wave ([Migration: Get started](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-getting-started), [Landing zone design](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/landing-zones)).
- **Zero downtime for every workload.** Backend and batch workloads can often accept a cutover window, which is cheaper ([Assess and discover](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)).
- **Rebuild for off-the-shelf software.** Rebuild means that you rewrite the app ([Migration: Get started](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-getting-started)).
- **A TCO without network costs.** Network use is a commonly overlooked migration cost ([Assess and discover](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-gcp-assessing-and-discovering-your-workloads)).
- **Rightsizing from a short sample.** Collect data over several days ([Discovery and assessment](https://docs.cloud.google.com/migration-center/docs/discovery-and-assessment-overview)).
- **Turning off the source right after the cutover.** Keep it as a fallback until the retirement criteria are met ([Validating a migration plan](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/migration-to-google-cloud-best-practices)).
