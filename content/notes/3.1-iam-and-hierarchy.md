## Why it matters

An architect decides who can do what, and at which level of the resource hierarchy each control attaches. On Google Cloud, child resources inherit most controls from their parents. The exam tests least privilege: the right role, at the right level, for the right principal (usually a group or a dedicated service account). It also tests the guardrails that block access even when a role grants it.

## Key points

- The hierarchy is organization, then folders, then projects, then resources. Allow, deny, and organization policies are inherited down the hierarchy ([About resource hierarchy](https://docs.cloud.google.com/resource-manager/docs/cloud-platform-resource-hierarchy)).
- The effective allow policy of a resource is the union of its own allow policy and the allow policies that it inherits ([Using resource hierarchy for access control](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/resource-hierarchy-access-control)).
- To take away an inherited permission, use a deny policy. IAM always checks deny policies before allow policies ([Deny policies](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/deny-overview)).
- IAM evaluates principal access boundary policies, deny policies, and allow policies. If any one of them blocks the request, IAM denies access ([IAM policy types](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/policy-types)).
- Legacy basic roles (Owner, Editor, Viewer) include thousands of permissions. Do not grant them in production unless no alternative exists ([Roles overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/roles-overview)).
- You can create custom roles only in an organization or a project, not in a folder. You must maintain them yourself ([Roles overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/roles-overview)).
- Grant roles to groups that model job functions, and manage group membership in your identity provider ([Authentication and authorization](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/blueprints/security-foundations/authentication-authorization)).
- IAM Conditions add rules such as an expiry time or a resource tag. You cannot use conditions with legacy basic roles or with `allUsers` and `allAuthenticatedUsers` ([Overview of IAM Conditions](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/conditions-overview)).
- Use a dedicated service account for each application. Some default service accounts get the Editor role on the project, which is too broad to share ([Best practices for using service accounts securely](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/best-practices-service-accounts)).
- Organizations created on or after May 3, 2024 enforce a security baseline. The baseline blocks service account key creation and key upload ([Manage Google Cloud security baseline constraints](https://docs.cloud.google.com/resource-manager/docs/manage-baseline-constraints)).
- Policy Intelligence finds unused access (role recommendations), shows who can access what (Policy Analyzer), and explains one access decision (Policy Troubleshooter) ([Policy Intelligence overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/policy-intelligence/docs/overview)).
- An organization resource requires a Google Workspace or Cloud Identity account. Each such account has exactly one organization resource ([About resource hierarchy](https://docs.cloud.google.com/resource-manager/docs/cloud-platform-resource-hierarchy)).

## Design the resource hierarchy

With an organization resource, projects belong to the company and not to the employee who created them. Folders need an organization resource ([About resource hierarchy](https://docs.cloud.google.com/resource-manager/docs/cloud-platform-resource-hierarchy)).

| Level | What it is for | Design rule |
|---|---|---|
| Organization | The root node for one company. Policies here apply to every resource. | Grant roles here to central teams, such as a network team that manages all networks. |
| Folder | Groups projects by environment, department, or team. Lets you delegate administration. | When a group needs a role in several projects, grant it on the folder. |
| Project | A trust boundary. Services in the same project have a default level of trust. | Put resources that share a trust boundary in the same project. |
| Resource | One topic, bucket, VM, or service account. | Grant at the smallest scope, such as Pub/Sub Publisher on one topic. |

Sources: [About resource hierarchy](https://docs.cloud.google.com/resource-manager/docs/cloud-platform-resource-hierarchy), [Using resource hierarchy for access control](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/resource-hierarchy-access-control), [Organization structure](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/blueprints/security-foundations/organization-structure).

The enterprise foundations blueprint (formerly the security foundations blueprint) is Google's reference design. It groups projects into environment folders: `bootstrap`, `common`, `production`, `nonproduction`, `development`, and `networking` ([Organization structure](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/blueprints/security-foundations/organization-structure)). People get view-only access, and a Cloud Build pipeline deploys all foundation changes ([Enterprise foundations blueprint](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/blueprints/security-foundations)).

## How IAM decides access

Three policy types decide access to most resources. Allow policies grant access. Deny policies and principal access boundary policies take access away. A fourth type, access policies (Preview), can allow or deny access to Eventarc resources ([IAM policy types](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/policy-types), [Control publishing access](https://docs.cloud.google.com/eventarc/advanced/docs/control-publishing-access)).

| Policy type | Attach it to | What it says | Use it for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Allow policy | One resource. Each resource has only one allow policy. | These principals have these roles on this resource and its descendants. | Normal grants. |
| Deny policy | An organization, folder, or project. | These principals cannot use these permissions, with optional exceptions. | Guardrails that override any grant. |
| Principal access boundary policy | A principal set, through a policy binding. | These principals can access only resources in this boundary. | Stop principals from using resources in other organizations. |

Sources: [IAM policy types](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/policy-types), [Deny policies](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/deny-overview), [Principal access boundary policies](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/principal-access-boundary-policies).

IAM evaluates the policy types at the same time. It helps to think of the evaluation in three stages ([IAM policy types](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/policy-types)):

1. Principal access boundary policies. If the principal is not eligible to access the resource, IAM denies access.
2. Deny policies on the resource and its ancestors. If a deny rule blocks a required permission, IAM denies access.
3. Allow policies on the resource and its ancestors. If no role grants the required permission, IAM denies access.

Deny policies are the tool for exceptions. The docs list three use cases, including these two ([Deny policies](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/deny-overview)):

- **Centralize admin privileges.** At the organization, deny custom role management to everyone except a `custom-role-admins` group.
- **Make an exception to an inherited grant.** Grant Service Account Key Admin on a folder. Then deny key creation on the production project only.

A deny rule can name exception principals and exception permissions. A denial condition can use only resource tags. If the condition cannot be evaluated, the deny rule applies ([Deny policies](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/deny-overview)).

Principal access boundary policies work in the other direction. They are attached to principal sets and limit what those principals can do. A use case is to keep service accounts limited to certain projects. These policies fail closed: if IAM cannot evaluate them, it blocks access ([Principal access boundary policies](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/principal-access-boundary-policies)).

## Choose the role type

| Role type | Use it when | Do not use it when |
|---|---|---|
| Legacy basic roles: Owner, Editor, Viewer | Rarely. Keep Owner for at least two principals on each project. | In production. These roles include thousands of permissions and cannot take conditions. |
| Predefined roles | Default choice. Google maintains them and adds permissions for new features. | The role contains much more than the task needs. |
| Custom roles | No predefined role gives least privilege. | You cannot maintain the role. |

Sources: [Roles overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/roles-overview), [Overview of IAM Conditions](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/conditions-overview), [Using resource hierarchy for access control](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/resource-hierarchy-access-control).

Facts about custom roles that the exam uses ([Roles overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/roles-overview)):

- You can grant a custom role only in the project or organization where you created it. To use a custom role in a folder, create it at the organization level.
- Some permissions are `NOT_SUPPORTED` in custom roles. A `TESTING` permission can behave in unexpected ways, so do not use it in production.
- A principal who can edit custom roles can add any permission to them. Give that ability only to a small number of trusted principals.

The newer basic roles Admin, Writer, and Reader (Preview) need the same caution ([Roles overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/roles-overview)).

## Principals: prefer groups and dedicated identities

| Principal | Identifier in an allow policy | Design note |
|---|---|---|
| Google Account | `user:alex@example.com` | A person. Prefer group grants. |
| Service account | `serviceAccount:app@my-project.iam.gserviceaccount.com` | A workload. Use one for each application. |
| Google group | `group:dba@example.com` | Cannot sign in. Change access by changing membership. |
| Domain | `domain:example.com` | All accounts in the Cloud Identity or Workspace account. |
| `allUsers` | `allUsers` | Anyone on the internet. Avoid for private data. |

Sources: [IAM principals](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/principals-overview).

Federated principals from workforce and workload identity pools are covered in [Secure remote and workload access](note:3.1-secure-access).

## IAM Conditions and temporary access

IAM Conditions give attribute-based access control. Common attributes are the request time, the resource name or type, and resource tags ([Overview of IAM Conditions](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/conditions-overview)). An example is temporary access to resolve a production issue:

```text
request.time < timestamp('2026-10-01T00:00:00Z')
```

Rules to remember ([Overview of IAM Conditions](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/conditions-overview)):

- A conditional binding does not override an unconditional binding for the same role. If the principal already has the role without a condition, the condition has no effect.
- To use conditions in the allow policy of a Cloud Storage bucket, enable uniform bucket-level access on the bucket.
- Deny rules support conditions, but only on resource tags.

For repeated requests for elevated access, use Privileged Access Manager (PAM). An entitlement defines who can request which roles, for how long, and whether a justification or approval is necessary. PAM revokes the roles when the grant ends, and audit logs record the access ([Privileged Access Manager overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/pam-overview)).

| Need | Choose |
|---|---|
| One known time window for one principal | A conditional role binding with an expiry time |
| Emergency or on-demand elevation, with justification and approval | A PAM entitlement |
| Access only to resources with a tag, such as `env: prod` | A condition on resource tags |

Sources: [Overview of IAM Conditions](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/conditions-overview), [Privileged Access Manager overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/pam-overview).

## Design service accounts

First decide if the workload needs a service account at all. Google recommends that you avoid service account keys whenever possible ([Best practices for using service accounts securely](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/best-practices-service-accounts)).

| Where the code runs | Recommended identity |
|---|---|
| Your workstation or Cloud Shell | Your user credentials, or impersonate a service account |
| On Google Cloud (VM, Cloud Run, and others) | Attach a dedicated service account to the resource. |
| On GKE | Workload Identity Federation for GKE. |
| Outside Google Cloud, with an IdP that supports federation | Workload Identity Federation. |
| Outside Google Cloud, with no federation support | A service account key, as the last option. |

Sources: [Best practices for using service accounts securely](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/best-practices-service-accounts).

Design rules ([Best practices for using service accounts securely](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/best-practices-service-accounts)):

- **One service account for each application.** When applications share a service account, audit logs cannot show which application made a change.
- **Do not use default service accounts.** Compute Engine and App Engine create default service accounts that can get the Editor role. The `constraints/iam.automaticIamGrantsForDefaultServiceAccounts` constraint stops the automatic grant. It does not remove the Editor role from existing default service accounts.
- **Do not rely on access scopes.** Scopes are coarse. For example, a scope cannot limit access to one bucket. Use fine-grained allow policies instead.
- **Grant access on the service account, not on the project.** Service Account Token Creator on a project lets the user impersonate every service account in that project.
- **Treat keys as a last resort.** Anyone who has a key can use it. No other authentication is necessary.

How impersonation and short-lived credentials work is covered in [Secure remote and workload access](note:3.1-secure-access). The constraints that block key creation are covered in [Organization policy, VPC Service Controls, and audit logging](note:3.1-security-controls).

## Find and fix excess access with Policy Intelligence

| Question | Tool |
|---|---|
| Which granted permissions does this principal not use? | Role recommendations (IAM recommender). It compares granted permissions with the permissions used in the last 90 days. |
| Who has access to this resource? What can this user access? | Policy Analyzer for allow policies (part of Cloud Asset Inventory) |
| Why does this principal have, or not have, this permission? | Policy Troubleshooter |
| What happens if I change this policy? | Policy Simulator (for allow, deny, and principal access boundary policies, and for organization policies) |
| Which service accounts are not used? | Service account insights and Activity Analyzer |
| Which service accounts can impersonate service accounts in other projects? | Lateral movement insights |

Sources: [Policy Intelligence overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/policy-intelligence/docs/overview), [Overview of role recommendations](https://docs.cloud.google.com/policy-intelligence/docs/role-recommendations-overview).

Role recommendations for basic roles have no charge. Advanced IAM recommender features need the Premium or Enterprise (deprecated) tier of Security Command Center ([Overview of role recommendations](https://docs.cloud.google.com/policy-intelligence/docs/role-recommendations-overview), [Security Command Center service tiers](https://docs.cloud.google.com/security-command-center/docs/service-tiers)).

Policy Troubleshooter checks allow and deny policies. Support for principal access boundary policies is in Preview. It does not check Cloud Storage ACLs or VPC Service Controls ([Troubleshoot IAM permissions](https://docs.cloud.google.com/policy-intelligence/docs/troubleshoot-access)).

## Separation of duties

Separation of duties means that no single principal has all the permissions for a harmful action ([Separation of duties](https://docs.cloud.google.com/kms/docs/separation-of-duties)). Patterns that the exam uses:

| Duty to separate | Google-recommended pattern |
|---|---|
| Key management and key use | Key managers (Cloud KMS Admin) do not get the CryptoKey Encrypter/Decrypter role. For CMEK, only the service agent uses the key. |
| Key storage | A central team manages keys in dedicated key projects, often one for each environment folder. Keys can also be in the same project as the data, if IAM roles stay strictly separate. |
| Changes to the foundation | People get view-only roles. A CI/CD pipeline with privileged service accounts makes changes. |
| Identity administration and cloud administration | The Workspace or Cloud Identity super admin grants Organization Administrator to other users, and does not administer Google Cloud daily. |

Sources: [Separation of duties](https://docs.cloud.google.com/kms/docs/separation-of-duties), [Enterprise foundations blueprint](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/blueprints/security-foundations), [About resource hierarchy](https://docs.cloud.google.com/resource-manager/docs/cloud-platform-resource-hierarchy).

Key management details are in [Encryption, Cloud KMS, and secrets](note:3.1-data-protection-kms).

## Identity foundation: Cloud Identity, SSO, and directory sync

Cloud Identity is an identity as a service (IDaaS) product that manages users and groups. It can federate with Active Directory and Microsoft Entra ID. Cloud Identity users do not get Google Workspace applications ([Overview of Cloud Identity](https://docs.cloud.google.com/identity/docs/overview), [IAM principals](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/principals-overview)).

| Existing identity system | Reference architecture |
|---|---|
| No on-premises IdP, and you use Google Workspace | Google as the IdP and the authoritative source |
| Active Directory | Active Directory as the IdP and authoritative source: sync with Google Cloud Directory Sync (GCDS), and SSO with SAML |
| Entra ID connected to Active Directory | Entra ID as the IdP, with Active Directory as the authoritative source |

Sources: [Reference architectures](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/identity/reference-architectures).

How federation with Active Directory works ([Federate Google Cloud with Active Directory](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/identity/federating-gcp-with-active-directory-introduction)):

- GCDS is a free Google tool. It provisions users and groups in one direction only: from Active Directory to Google.
- Provisioning does not include passwords. Active Directory stays the only system that manages credentials.
- For SSO, Google delegates authentication to Active Directory through SAML, for example with AD FS. The user must be provisioned before the first sign-on.

Best practices for federation ([Best practices for federating Google Cloud with an external identity provider](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/identity/best-practices-for-federating)):

- Make the external IdP the source of truth. Create, change, and delete users only there, and automate provisioning.
- Create dedicated super-admin accounts, such as `alice-admin@example.com`, and use them only when necessary.
- Disable SSO for super-admin users, or enforce post-SSO verification for them.


## AWS mapping

| AWS | Google Cloud | Difference that matters |
|---|---|---|
| AWS Organizations | Resource Manager (organization, folders, projects) | Allow, deny, and organization policies attach to hierarchy nodes and are inherited. The project is the trust boundary. |
| Amazon Identity and Access Management | Identity and Access Management (IAM) | You attach an allow policy to a resource, and it names the principals. A role is a collection of permissions. Google maintains predefined roles, and you can create custom roles. |
| AWS IAM Identity Center | Cloud Identity | An organization resource requires a Cloud Identity or Workspace account. Users sign in with SSO to your IdP. |

Sources: [Compare AWS and Azure services to Google Cloud](https://docs.cloud.google.com/docs/get-started/aws-azure-gcp-service-comparison), [Using resource hierarchy for access control](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/resource-hierarchy-access-control), [IAM policy types](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/policy-types), [About resource hierarchy](https://docs.cloud.google.com/resource-manager/docs/cloud-platform-resource-hierarchy), [Roles overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/roles-overview), [Authentication and authorization](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/blueprints/security-foundations/authentication-authorization).

## Exam traps

- **"Add a more restrictive allow policy on the project."** Allow policies are a union. A lower-level allow policy cannot remove an inherited grant. Use a deny policy, or move the grant lower ([Using resource hierarchy for access control](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/resource-hierarchy-access-control), [Deny policies](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/deny-overview)).
- **"Use the Compute Engine default service account."** It can have the Editor role and is shared by many workloads. Create a dedicated service account with only the roles it needs ([Best practices for using service accounts securely](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/best-practices-service-accounts)).
- **"Grant Service Account Token Creator on the project."** This lets the user impersonate every service account in the project. Grant it on one service account ([Best practices for using service accounts securely](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/best-practices-service-accounts)).
- **"Create a custom role on the folder."** Custom roles exist only at the organization or project level ([Roles overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/roles-overview)).
- **"Add a time condition to the user's existing role."** If an unconditional binding for the same role exists, the condition has no effect. Remove the unconditional binding ([Overview of IAM Conditions](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/conditions-overview)).
- **"Use Policy Troubleshooter to explain a VPC Service Controls denial."** Policy Troubleshooter does not diagnose VPC Service Controls or Cloud Storage ACLs ([Troubleshoot IAM permissions](https://docs.cloud.google.com/policy-intelligence/docs/troubleshoot-access)).
- **"Sync passwords to Google with GCDS."** GCDS does not sync passwords. Users authenticate to the IdP through SAML SSO ([Federate Google Cloud with Active Directory](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/identity/federating-gcp-with-active-directory-introduction)).
- **"Grant the Owner role with an expiry condition."** You cannot add conditions to legacy basic roles. Use a predefined role with a condition, or PAM ([Overview of IAM Conditions](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/conditions-overview)).
